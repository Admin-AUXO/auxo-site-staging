One-minute reads on data and AI.

Short, sourced positions for finance, operations, engineering, hiring, sales and content teams.

Foundations
Guardrails
  1. Data

    Your numbers aren't messy. They're scattered.

    Most teams already hold the data they need. The work is making the systems agree, so one figure means one thing.

    Data

    Nobody’s numbers are wrong, exactly. They just don’t match. Sales reports one revenue figure, finance books another, and the board hears a third. That isn’t a shortage of data. It is three systems that never agreed to mean the same thing. Collecting more won’t fix it. Making the systems you already run reconcile will. Hand-fixing at month-end is the symptom. Agreement built in upstream is the cure, and it is also what any AI tool needs before its answers can be trusted.

    Where this fits: Build. One source your team and your AI tools can rely on.

  2. Data

    The data already lives in systems you run.

    Between your CRM, your accounting system, your HR tool and your product data, the raw material is already there. The gap is connection, not collection.

    Data

    Before you buy another platform, open the cupboards. Your CRM knows the customers. Accounting knows the money. Your HR system knows the people, and your engineering tools know what was built and shipped. The raw material isn’t missing. It has just never been connected. A new platform resets the clock, retrains everyone and bets the year on a migration. The quieter win is connecting what you already pay for, so it finally talks and your AI tools can read it.

    Where this fits: Build. Built from the systems you already run, and yours to keep.

  3. Data

    Agree what a number means before you build the dashboard.

    Many reporting disputes aren't about the data. They're about whether 'active customer' or 'headcount' means the same thing to two teams.

    Data

    Most reporting fights aren’t about numbers. They’re about words. Is an “active customer” someone who paid this month, or someone who logged in? Does “headcount” include contractors? Give two teams one term and two definitions, and no dashboard will settle it. It will just show the disagreement faster. An AI assistant will do the same, with more confidence. The plain move works: agree the definitions, write them down, and let the data follow the words. Definitions first. Charts later.

    Where this fits: Advise. Agreed definitions and owners, so one figure means one thing.

  4. Data

    The slow part isn't the model. It's the cleanup.

    Teams budget for the AI and forget the weeks spent reconciling what they already have. Get the foundation right and the rest is quick.

    Data

    Everyone budgets for the AI model and forgets the cleanup that comes first. The model is rarely the hard part. The hard part is connecting systems that were never built to talk to each other, and agreeing which record is right. Skip it and even a strong model gives wrong answers quickly. Do it once, cleaned, agreed and connected, and every project after it gets faster. The foundation isn’t the dull part of the work. It’s the part that makes the rest possible.

    Where this fits: Analyze. Find what needs cleaning before you spend on AI.

  5. Data

    Your AI assistant can see what your staff can see.

    Workplace AI assistants answer from the files each person can already open. Loose sharing settings become AI answers. Fix access before rollout.

    Microsoft · Secure data foundation for Copilot ↗
    Data

    Workplace AI assistants answer from the data each person is already allowed to open. That sounds safe until you look at how files are actually shared. The salary sheet in an open folder. The board pack shared with the whole company. Before, nobody found them. Now anyone can ask. Microsoft’s own deployment blueprint for Copilot starts with fixing oversharing. The lesson holds for any assistant or agent: tidy who can see what first, then switch AI on.

    Where this fits: Analyze. Check access and data readiness before AI goes live.

  6. Data

    Pick one system of record. Then reconcile to it.

    When two systems disagree, someone has to decide which one is right. Name it once for each kind of data, and reconcile everything else to it.

    Data

    Every company has a “whose number is right” argument. Customer counts differ between the CRM and billing. Headcount differs between HR and payroll. The fix isn’t a better spreadsheet. It’s a decision: for each kind of data, one system is the record, and everything else reconciles to it. Write that down, give it an owner, and check it on a schedule. Your team stops arguing about sources, and your AI tools stop reading two answers to the same question.

    Where this fits: Advise. Clear owners and a named record for each kind of data.

  7. Reporting

    The board doesn't want a dashboard. It wants a decision.

    Reporting earns its place when it settles a question in the room, not when it adds one more screen no one opens between meetings.

    Reporting

    A board or investor update isn’t there to show charts. It’s there to decide something: approve this hire, cut that spend, defend a number next week. So build the report backwards from the decision it serves, and most of the clutter goes. The test for any chart is simple: did it settle a question, or open another tab nobody clicks? Keep the few figures that move the decision. Everything else is decoration you pay to maintain.

    Where this fits: Build. Reporting that holds up when the board, investors or auditors ask hard questions.

  8. Reporting

    One source of truth beats five tidy ones.

    Every clean report built on its own copy of the data is one more version of the truth to argue about. Agree the figure once, upstream.

    Reporting

    Five tidy reports, each built on its own copy of the data, are five versions of the truth. They agree until the quarter that matters. Then they split, and the argument moves into a long email thread that never quite ends. One number everyone reads from beats five neat ones nobody fully trusts. Agree the figure once, upstream, and point every report and every AI tool at the same place. Reconciliation should be a design choice made once, not a monthly ritual.

    Where this fits: Build. One source your team and your AI tools can rely on.

  9. Reporting

    Sustainability is becoming board reporting, not a side report.

    IFRS S1 and S2 treat sustainability figures as information investors use to make decisions. Where they apply, those numbers need the same care as the accounts.

    IFRS S1 General Requirements ↗
    Reporting

    For years, sustainability figures sat in a booklet nobody audited. The ISSB standards, IFRS S1 and S2, treat them as information investors use to decide where money goes. They took effect for reporting periods from 1 January 2024, and each country decides whether to adopt them. Even where they don’t apply yet, large customers and lenders ask similar questions. The teams that cope well stop treating these numbers as a yearly scramble. They source them like revenue: once, traceably, from the systems of record.

    Where this fits: Build. Numbers sourced once and traced back, ready when investors ask.

  10. Reporting

    A good pack answers the next question before it's asked.

    Investors don't reward pretty slides. They reward numbers they can trace to source without a follow-up email.

    Reporting

    The moment that tests a report isn’t the meeting. It’s the question twenty minutes later: where did this come from? Does it tie to the accounts? What changed since last quarter? A pack that answers those in one click earns trust on the spot. One that triggers a week of reconciliation loses it just as fast. Write for the question behind the question, and a funding round or an audit stops being an event you brace for. The answers are already there.

    Where this fits: Build. Numbers that trace back to source and hold up under questions.

  11. Reporting

    Say what your AI does, and no more.

    Regulators already treat false AI claims as misleading statements. Describe the AI you actually run, in plain terms you can back up.

    SEC · AI washing charges (2024) ↗
    Reporting

    In March 2024 the US SEC settled charges against two investment advisers for false and misleading statements about their use of AI. The SEC chair called it “AI washing”. The lesson reaches well beyond finance. Every claim about AI in a deck, on a website or in an investor update is a statement someone may check. Describe what the AI actually does, on what data, and who approves its output. If you can’t show it working, don’t claim it.

    Where this fits: Analyze. An honest read of what your data and AI can do today.

  12. Reporting

    Due diligence is a data test.

    Whether you are raising, selling or signing a large customer, the other side will ask for numbers. How fast you can trace them says a lot.

    Reporting

    Every funding round, acquisition and large contract ends in the same place: a data room and a list of questions. How many customers renewed? What does each one cost to serve? Does the forecast tie to the accounts? A team with one traced source answers in a day. A team stitching spreadsheets answers in weeks, and the delay itself becomes a concern. Get the data ready before anyone asks, and due diligence becomes a check, not a crisis.

    Where this fits: Analyze. See where your numbers would hold up, and where they wouldn’t.

  13. Forecasting

    Forecasts are for planning, not for promising.

    A range you can defend to an investor beats a single confident number you can't. Show the assumptions, and the conversation changes.

    Forecasting

    A single confident number invites an argument you can’t win, because everyone knows a point estimate will be wrong. A range you can defend, with the assumptions in plain view, turns a forecast into a planning tool. The honesty is the point, not the apology. Investors and boards trust the team that shows its working far more than the one hoping its single number lands. The same goes for any forecast an AI tool produces: ask what it assumed. Forecast to plan, not to promise.

    Where this fits: Build. Forecasts and reports that hold up under questions.

  14. Forecasting

    Anchor your forecast to something others can check.

    A forecast built only on internal hopes is easy to argue down. Tie the key assumptions to sources an investor or lender can check for themselves.

    Forecasting

    Every forecast rests on a few assumptions that do most of the work: how fast you hire, how many customers renew, what prices do, what a supplier will charge. If those come from gut feel, the first sharp question takes them apart. If each one points to a source someone else can check, such as your own history, a signed contract or a published benchmark, the forecast is much harder to wave away. Name the source next to each assumption. It takes minutes and saves meetings.

    Where this fits: Build. Forecasts tied to sources your board and investors can check.

  15. Forecasting

    Plan in ranges, pressure-test with scenarios.

    One line on a chart hides the risk. A base case, a downside, and what would have to be true for each turns a forecast into a decision tool.

    Forecasting

    One line on a chart tells you what someone hopes. A base case, a downside, and a note on what would have to be true for each tell you what to do when things change, which they will. This isn’t hedging. It answers the question that matters before you commit money or people: what breaks this plan, and how would we see it coming? It works for a hiring plan, a cash runway or a product launch alike. Plan in ranges. Test with scenarios.

    Where this fits: Build. Forecasts that show the range and the assumptions behind it.

  16. Forecasting

    Forecast the cash, not just the revenue.

    Bookings look good on a slide. Payment terms, late invoices and timing of costs decide whether the quarter actually works.

    Forecasting

    Revenue writes the headline. Cash decides whether the quarter works. A forecast that tracks bookings but waves through payment terms, late invoices and the timing of payroll and suppliers shows how the business looks in a photo, not how it behaves in motion. Most of these figures already sit in your billing and accounting systems, so they don’t need to be guessed. Model the money moving in and out, week by week. That is the number that tells you how much runway you really have.

    Where this fits: Build. Forecasts grounded in cash, built from the systems you already run.

  17. Forecasting

    AI running costs are a forecast input, not a footnote.

    Agents use far more model capacity than a chat window. Anthropic reports about 4 times the tokens for agents and about 15 times for multi-agent systems.

    Anthropic · Multi-agent research system ↗
    Forecasting

    AI budgets usually count the licence and forget the running cost. Agents read more, think in more steps and call more tools than a chat window. Anthropic’s own engineering write-up reports that agents typically use about 4 times the tokens of a chat, and multi-agent systems about 15 times. That can be worth it for valuable work, and wasteful for simple work. So model usage like any other variable cost: by task, by volume, by month. Then pick the simplest setup that does the job.

    Where this fits: Advise. The right AI setup for each task, with the running costs in view.

  18. Forecasting

    Put the regulatory dates in the plan.

    New rules on automated decisions come with fixed start dates. California's apply from 1 January 2027. Plan the work before the deadline, not at it.

    California Privacy Protection Agency · ADMT rules ↗
    Forecasting

    Rules on AI and automated decisions now arrive with dates attached. California’s privacy regulator says businesses that use automated decision-making technology to make significant decisions must meet its new requirements from 1 January 2027. Other places have their own timelines. Each one means work: listing the tools, writing notices, checking how decisions are made. That work takes people and weeks. Put the dates in the plan and the budget now, so the deadline is a checkpoint and not a scramble.

    Where this fits: Advise. Governance planned around the dates that apply to you.

  19. AI

    AI nobody checks is a liability, not an asset.

    Put automation where the work is repetitive and reversible, and keep a person signing off where the stakes are real. That's how it survives an audit.

    NIST AI Risk Management Framework ↗
    AI

    Automation earns its keep where the work is repetitive and a mistake is cheap to undo. Where the stakes are real, keep a person signing off, and keep a clear record of what ran, on what data, and why. NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework, which is free and voluntary, builds this into its Govern and Measure functions. It’s also the version that survives an audit and the version your own team trusts. Unchecked AI doesn’t save time. It postpones the day someone has to explain what it did.

    Where this fits: Build. AI agents carry the routine work. A person approves what matters.

  20. AI

    Start where a mistake is cheap.

    The first thing you automate shouldn't be a payment, a refund or a hiring decision. Earn trust on low-stakes, reversible work first.

    OpenAI · A practical guide to building agents ↗
    AI

    The first thing you automate shouldn’t be the thing that can’t be undone. Start where being wrong is cheap: drafting, sorting, matching, routine reports. Let a track record build before an agent touches anything that bites back. OpenAI’s own guide to building agents says sensitive, irreversible or high-stakes actions, such as refunds or payments, should go to a person until the agent has proved reliable. By the time you reach the work that matters, the system has earned some trust. Ambition is fine. Sequence it.

    Where this fits: Build. Agents start on routine work, and a person approves what goes out.

  21. AI

    Know which of your AI would count as 'high-risk'.

    Tools that sort CVs or score credit sit in the EU AI Act's high-risk group. Treat them differently from a drafting assistant.

    EU AI Act ↗
    AI

    Not all AI carries the same weight. A tool that drafts an email is housekeeping. A tool that sorts job applicants or scores someone’s credit is something else. The EU AI Act names CV-sorting software and credit scoring as examples of high-risk uses, with duties such as human oversight and activity logs. So sort your AI by consequence, not by how clever it feels. Keep it light where the output is a suggestion. Add full governance, and a person’s decision, where it decides who gets through the door.

    Where this fits: Advise. Clear rules for each AI use, matched to what is at stake.

  22. AI

    AI you can't see, you can't govern.

    Most teams run more AI than leadership knows, spread across personal accounts, plugins and vendor features. You can't set rules for what you haven't listed.

    NIST AI Risk Management Framework ↗
    AI

    The AI that should worry you usually isn’t the one you approved. It’s the personal chatbot account someone pastes customer data into, the plugin someone switched on, the vendor feature nobody logged. You can’t govern what you haven’t listed, which is why NIST’s framework includes a Map function: know what is running, on whose data, deciding what. Then write the policy. The list is dull, and it is the cheapest control you will ever build. Visibility first. Rules second.

    Where this fits: Advise. Know what AI runs, on what data, and who owns it.

  23. AI

    The EU AI Act can reach a desk outside the EU.

    If your AI's output is used in the EU, you can be in scope even with no EU office. The rules follow your customers, not your address.

    EU AI Act · Article 2 (scope) ↗
    AI

    “We’re not an EU company” is no longer the end of the sentence. Article 2 of the EU AI Act covers providers and deployers based outside the EU where the output of their AI system is used in the EU. A team in Dubai, New York or London can be in scope without an office in Brussels. If you have European customers, users or staff, the rules follow your output, not your address. Map where your AI’s results land before you grow, not after.

    Where this fits: Advise. AI set up with the cross-border rules in view from the start.

  24. AI

    Govern, Map, Measure, Manage. Borrow the checklist.

    You don't need to invent AI governance. NIST's four functions give a free, recognised structure that regulators and investors already understand.

    NIST AI Risk Management Framework ↗
    AI

    AI governance sounds like a committee and a year you don’t have. It doesn’t have to start that way. NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework is free, voluntary, and built on four functions anyone can adopt: Govern (who is accountable), Map (what is running), Measure (does it work) and Manage (act on what you find). A recognised framework also travels well. An auditor, investor or enterprise customer knows the terms already. Borrow the structure, then fit it to how your team already works.

    Where this fits: Advise. Governance built on a recognised framework, sized to your team.

  25. Compliance

    Compliance is cheaper as a habit than a scramble.

    The real cost isn't the filing or the audit. It's the week your best people lose rebuilding the same evidence before every deadline.

    Compliance

    The expensive part of compliance was never the filing. It’s the week your best people lose rebuilding the same evidence by hand before every audit, investor review or customer security questionnaire. Build the evidence into how the business runs: access logs, approvals, data lists, kept as the work happens. Then the deadline stops being something anyone dreads. It becomes a date the work is already done by. The goal isn’t heroics near the cut-off. It’s arriving at it calm, with everything where a reviewer can find it.

    Where this fits: Advise. Clear rules for your data and AI, so you’re ready when auditors, regulators or investors ask.

  26. Compliance

    AI in hiring has its own rules.

    In New York City, an automated hiring tool needs a bias audit within the past year, published results and notice to candidates. Check the rules where you hire.

    NYC · Automated employment decision tools ↗
    Compliance

    Using AI to screen applicants is not the same as using it to draft an email. New York City has enforced its law on automated employment decision tools since July 2023. An employer can’t use one unless it has had a bias audit within the past year, the results are public, and candidates get notice. The EU AI Act treats CV-sorting tools as high-risk too. Rules differ by place, so check them with your advisers. And keep a person making every hiring decision.

    Where this fits: Marketplace. Hiring tools set up with clear rules, and a person makes every hiring decision.

  27. Compliance

    Know where each AI tool sends your data.

    Every AI tool you add is a new place your data can go. Before you sign, know where it is stored, who can see it, and whether it trains a model.

    Compliance

    Signing up for an AI tool takes a minute. Knowing what it does with your data takes longer, and that is the part that matters. Where is the data stored? Who at the vendor can see it? Is it used to train their models? Can you export it and leave? A finance team pasting invoices, or a recruiter uploading CVs, is sharing regulated data with a third party. Ask the four questions before the contract, not after the first customer asks you.

    Where this fits: Marketplace. Every listed tool is checked for how it handles your data.

  28. Compliance

    The audit trail should be a by-product, not a project.

    The EU AI Act expects high-risk systems to log activity so results can be traced. If you have to reconstruct the trail, you never really had it.

    EU AI Act ↗
    Compliance

    When someone asks “show us how this decision was made”, the answer should already exist. For high-risk systems, the EU AI Act lists logging of activity so results can be traced. Auditors, enterprise customers and investors increasingly expect the same of any AI that touches money or people. If your answer is a project to rebuild the trail, you didn’t have a trail. Record who did what, when, on what data and with whose approval, as the work happens. The best audit prep is the one you never have to do.

    Where this fits: Build. Every agent run recorded, with the person who approved it.

  29. Compliance

    Customer and staff data is regulated data.

    CRM records, payroll files and CVs are personal data under laws such as the UAE PDPL and the GDPR. AI tools that read them are covered too.

    UAE Personal Data Protection Law ↗
    Compliance

    Most teams under-rate how much personal data they hold. CRM histories, payroll files, CVs and support tickets are all personal data. In the UAE, the Personal Data Protection Law covers processing inside or outside the country, with rules on consent and cross-border transfer. In Europe and the UK, the GDPR sets similar duties. When an AI tool reads that data, those rules come with it. Know what personal data you hold, why, and who and what can touch it, before you connect an AI tool to it.

    Where this fits: Analyze. Know what personal data you hold before AI reads it.

  30. Compliance

    Data crosses borders. So do the rules.

    Personal data leaving Europe needs a legal basis for the transfer. Teams split across the UAE, the US and Europe should map where data flows.

    European Commission · International data transfers ↗
    Compliance

    A team in London, a vendor in California and an office in Dubai can mean three sets of data rules on one customer record. Personal data leaving Europe needs a transfer tool, such as an adequacy decision or standard contractual clauses. The UAE’s data law has its own rules on cross-border transfer. AI tools add another hop, because prompts and files often go to a model hosted somewhere else. The plain first step is a data map: what is held where, under which law, and which tools send it on.

    Where this fits: Advise. Data flows mapped, so every tool fits the rules that apply.

  31. Ownership

    Own the system, not just the slides.

    If your team can't run it the day after the outside help leaves, you didn't buy a capability. You rented a presentation.

    Ownership

    If your team can’t run it the morning after the outside help leaves, you didn’t buy a capability. You rented a convincing presentation. Real ownership means the code, the setup, the accounts, the documentation and the know-how all stay on your side, in your company’s name. No retainer to renew, no support ticket every time a number or an agent rule needs changing. The point of doing the work properly is to leave you more capable, not more dependent.

    Where this fits: Build. Set up end to end and handed over. Built to last, and yours to keep.

  32. Ownership

    No lock-in means no black box.

    A system you can't see inside is a system you don't really own. If you can't explain how a number or an AI output was produced, you can't defend it.

    NIST AI Risk Management Framework ↗
    Ownership

    Lock-in isn’t only the contract you can’t leave. It’s the quieter trap where nobody on your side can explain how the thing works: an agent whose rules only the vendor knows, a pipeline nobody internal has read. NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework treats explainability as part of what makes AI trustworthy. Real ownership means you can trace any number or AI output back to its source and explain it to an auditor or investor without calling anyone. If you can’t see inside it, you don’t own it.

    Where this fits: Build. Systems you can see into, explain and run yourself.

  33. Ownership

    The best fix rarely means ripping things out.

    Rip-and-replace resets the clock and bets the outcome on a migration. The quieter win is making what you already pay for work together.

    Ownership

    Rip-and-replace sounds decisive in the meeting and rarely is in practice. It resets the clock, retrains everyone, and stakes the outcome on a migration going smoothly. The better win is usually to build on what you already run, your CRM, your accounting, your HR and engineering tools, and make them work together. If an AI tool or agent you already have works, keep it. Your people keep the tools they know, and a new platform becomes the last resort instead of the first move.

    Where this fits: Advise. We’ll tell you when not to build, and when not to replace.

  34. Ownership

    Documentation is the deliverable, not the afterthought.

    A system isn't really handed over until someone on your team can change it from the written word alone. Write it for them, not for engineers.

    Ownership

    A working system with no readable documentation is a single point of failure. It runs until the one person who understands it moves on. Handover isn’t done when the code runs or the agent sends its first draft. It’s done when someone on your team can change it from the written word alone. Write it plainly, for the people who will run it day to day: what it does, where the data comes from, who approves what, and how to change a rule. That document is the difference between a capability you keep and one that leaves.

    Where this fits: Build. Plain-English documentation and training, so the know-how stays with your team.

  35. Ownership

    If you can't explain the number, you can't defend it.

    Regulators, auditors and investors increasingly expect a traceable answer, not a confident one. Explainability is what holds up under questioning.

    ICO · Guidance on AI and data protection ↗
    Ownership

    The hardest moment for any number isn’t producing it. It’s being asked, live, exactly how you got it. Regulators are pushing the same way. The UK’s data protection regulator, the ICO, builds its AI guidance around accountability, transparency and accuracy. In practice, every figure or AI output you put in front of a lender, an auditor or a board should trace back to its source and its logic. A confident answer you can’t unpack is a liability. A traceable one ends the conversation.

    Where this fits: Build. Systems where every number and every AI output traces back to its source.

  36. Ownership

    Own the tests, not just the tool.

    An AI agent is only as trustworthy as the checks you run on it. Anthropic suggests starting with 20 to 50 simple tasks drawn from real failures.

    Anthropic · Demystifying evals for AI agents ↗
    Ownership

    Most teams test an AI agent once, like it, and switch it on. Then a model update or a new data source changes its behaviour, and nobody notices until a customer does. The fix is a set of checks you own: real tasks, with known good answers, run every time something changes. Anthropic’s guidance on evaluating agents says 20 to 50 simple tasks drawn from real failures is a good start. Keep those tests in your name, next to the agent. Then you can prove it still works, not just insist it does.

    Where this fits: Build. Agents handed over with their tests, and optional managed upkeep to keep them tuned.
